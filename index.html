Governance & Ministry of Health (MOH) BARMM

  • RA 11054 (Bangsamoro Organic Law) – established BARMM with fiscal and political autonomy.
  • Ministry of Health (MOH) BARMM – equivalent to DOH but under the Bangsamoro government. Led by the Minister of Health.
  • MOH BARMM has devolved powers over health facilities, regulation, and program implementation within the region.
  • Coordinates with DOH for national programs but has autonomous budget and policy‑making.

Geographic coverage:

Provinces: Maguindanao del Norte, Maguindanao del Sur, Lanao del Sur, Basilan, Sulu, Tawi‑Tawi; plus Special Geographic Area (6 municipalities of Cotabato).

Key distinction: Unlike regular LGUs under RA 7160, BARMM has a parliamentary system and the MOH directly manages regional hospitals and provincial health offices in coordination with provincial LGUs.

Health Facilities in BARMM

Primary Level

  • Barangay Health Stations (BHS) – often far apart, many without regular midwife.
  • Rural Health Units (RHUs) – under municipal LGUs, but many under‑staffed.
  • Community‑managed health posts (indigenous/ Moro communities).

Secondary Level

  • Provincial hospitals – e.g., Amai Pakpak Medical Center (Lanao del Sur), Cotabato Regional Medical Center (part of BARMM SGA), Basilan General Hospital, Sulu Provincial Hospital, Tawi‑Tawi Provincial Hospital.
  • District hospitals – limited capacity, often without surgical teams.

Tertiary / Regional Referral

  • Cotabato Regional and Medical Center (CRMC) – largest referral hospital for BARMM, DOH‑retained but co‑managed with MOH.
  • Amai Pakpak Medical Center – tertiary referral for Lanao del Sur and Marawi.

Challenge: Many municipalities have no hospital; patients rely on RHUs and BHS with limited supplies and personnel.

Unique Challenges & Health Barriers

  • Geographic isolation – islands (Basilan, Sulu, Tawi‑Tawi) and mountainous areas; transportation difficulties.
  • Armed conflict & peace and order – displacement of health workers, destroyed facilities, limited access during hostilities.
  • Hard‑to‑reach communities – some barangays accessible only by boat or long trek.
  • Shortage of health workers – low retention of doctors, nurses, midwives due to security concerns and lack of housing.
  • Cultural and religious sensitivities – need for gender‑sensitive care, respect for traditional healers (e.g., hilot, tambo).

Health indicators (worse than national average):

  • High maternal mortality ratio (MMR) – many home deliveries without skilled birth attendants.
  • High infant and under‑five mortality – malnutrition, vaccine‑preventable diseases.
  • Low immunization coverage – missed children due to conflict.
  • Malnutrition and food insecurity – especially in Sulu and Tawi‑Tawi.
  • Limited access to safe water and sanitation – diarrheal diseases common.

Priority Health Programs (MOH BARMM)

  • Maternal, Newborn and Child Health (MNCH) – BEmONC training for midwives, home visiting for high‑risk pregnancies.
  • Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) – “Reaching Every Barangay” strategy adapted for conflict zones; mobile vaccination teams.
  • TB Control Program (NTP) – DOTS expansion, challenges in sputum transport from islands.
  • Malaria and Filariasis elimination – endemic in Sulu, Tawi‑Tawi, and Maguindanao; indoor residual spraying and mass drug administration.
  • Schistosomiasis control – in areas with rice paddies.
  • Nutrition program – micronutrient supplementation, feeding programs in schools, management of severe acute malnutrition.
  • WASH (Water, Sanitation & Hygiene) – construction of toilets, promotion of safe water sources.

Bangsamoro UHC (Universal Health Care) – integration of BARMM into NHIP (PhilHealth) with subsidies for indigents.

Public Health Nurse in BARMM

  • Mobile service delivery: PHN leads outreach teams to isolated barangays via boat or habal‑habal.
  • Emergency preparedness: responds to conflict‑related displacements, sets up temporary health posts in evacuation centers.
  • Culturally competent care: respects Islamic practices, gender sensitivity (female health workers for female patients).
  • Coordination with MILF/Bangsamoro structures: engages with local community leaders (datus, imams) to promote health programs.
  • Training BHWs and traditional birth attendants (TBAs): promotes clean deliveries and referral of complications.
  • Surveillance and outbreak response: reports notifiable diseases despite logistical difficulties; uses satellite phones where needed.
  • Advocacy for peace and health: works with MOH BARMM and humanitarian organizations (ICRC, UNICEF).

Key partners: MOH BARMM, DOH‑CHD SOCCSKSARGEN (for SGA), WHO, UNICEF, IFRC, local NGOs.
